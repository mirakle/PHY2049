    Review: one component on an AC source

    With a single component across the source \(\mathcal{E}(t)=\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t)\), the component voltage equals the source voltage. Every current has the form \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t\pm\varphi)\), using \(+\) when the current is ahead of the voltage and \(-\) when it trails the voltage.

    One source, one component
    ComponentOpposition to currentCurrentPhasors
    Resistor \(R\)\(R\)\(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t)\), \(I=\dfrac{V_R}{R}\)Current and voltage phasors point the same way
    Capacitor \(C\)\(X_C=\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\)\(i(t)=I\cos\!\left(\omega t+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\), \(I=\dfrac{V_C}{X_C}\)Current phasor \(90^\circ\) ahead (counterclockwise) of the voltage

    “ELI the ICE man”

    ELI: in an inductor (L), the voltage (E) leads the current (I). ICE: in a capacitor (C), the current (I) leads the voltage (E). A resistor has no shift.

    The next checkpoint asks you to recognize these three phasor patterns.

    Checkpoint 1: match phasors to circuits

    The series RC circuit

    Two facts hold at every instant:

    • The components are in series, so the same current \(i(t)\) flows through the resistor and the capacitor.
    • The loop rule says the two voltages add up to the source voltage: \(v_R(t)+v_C(t)=\mathcal{E}(t)\).

    Adding two cosines with different phases directly is messy. Phasors make it a vector sum: each instantaneous value is the horizontal projection of its phasor, and the projection of a sum of vectors is the sum of the projections. So the source phasor is the vector sum of the resistor and capacitor phasors.

    The phasor method

    1. Sketch the phasor for the current \(I\). Its angle does not matter, so draw it at a low angle, around \(30^\circ\), for convenience.
    2. Sketch the resistor voltage phasor \(V_R\). Since \(v_R(t)=R\,i(t)\), it is always in step with the current: parallel to \(I\).
    3. Sketch the capacitor voltage phasor \(V_C\). The capacitor voltage trails the current by \(\pi/2\) (ICE), so \(V_C\) is \(90^\circ\) clockwise from \(I\).
    4. Add the two voltage phasors with the parallelogram rule. The sum is the source phasor, of length \(\mathcal{E}_0\).

    RC circuit: peak current and phase angle

    The phasor diagram is a right triangle with legs \(V_R\) and \(V_C\) and hypotenuse \(\mathcal{E}_0\).

    Peak current

    1. Peak voltages of each component: \(V_R=RI\) and \(V_C=X_C I\), with \(X_C=\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\).
    2. Pythagoras: \(\mathcal{E}_0=\sqrt{V_R^2+V_C^2}=\sqrt{(RI)^2+(X_C I)^2}=I\sqrt{R^2+X_C^2}\).
    3. Solve for the current: \[I=\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+X_C^2}}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\dfrac{1}{\omega^2C^2}}}\]

    The combination \(Z=\sqrt{R^2+X_C^2}\) plays the role of a resistance for the whole circuit, so \(I=\mathcal{E}_0/Z\). It is called the impedance and is measured in ohms. Note that the peak current depends on the frequency through \(X_C\).

    Phase angle

    1. The phase angle \(\varphi\) is the angle between the current phasor and the source phasor. In the triangle, \(\tan\varphi=\dfrac{V_C}{V_R}=\dfrac{X_C I}{RI}=\dfrac{X_C}{R}\).
    2. So \[\varphi=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{X_C}{R}\right)\]
    3. The current phasor is counterclockwise from the source phasor, so the current is ahead of the voltage and we use the \(+\) sign: \[i(t)=I\cos(\omega t+\varphi)\]

    About signs. Some notes write \(\varphi=\tan^{-1}(-X_C/R)\), a negative angle, inside \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t-\varphi)\). Two minus signs give the same result: \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t+|\varphi|)\). With our convention \(\varphi\) is always positive and the sign in front of it tells you who leads.

    Checks

    • No capacitor effect (\(X_C\to 0\)): \(I\to\mathcal{E}_0/R\) and \(\varphi\to 0\), the resistor-only result.
    • Capacitor dominates (\(X_C\gg R\)): \(\varphi\to 90^\circ\), the capacitor-only result.

    RC phasors in action

    The phasors rotate together, keeping their shape. On the graph, each current peak comes before the next source-voltage peak by \(\varphi/\omega\) seconds.

    Try this

    • Increase the capacitance or the frequency. \(X_C\) shrinks, the triangle flattens, and \(\varphi\) drops toward \(0\).
    • Make \(X_C\) equal to \(R\) and check that \(\varphi=45^\circ\).
    • Pause at any instant and check that \(v_R+v_C=\mathcal{E}\) in the readouts.

    How an RC circuit responds to frequency

    The peak current \(I=\dfrac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+1/(\omega^2C^2)}}\) depends on \(\omega=2\pi f\). Look at the two extremes.

    1. Low frequency, \(\omega\to 0\): \(\dfrac{1}{\omega^2C^2}\to\infty\) dominates the denominator, so \(I\approx\dfrac{\mathcal{E}_0}{1/(\omega C)}=\omega C\,\mathcal{E}_0\to 0\). Almost no current.
    2. High frequency, \(\omega\to\infty\): \(\dfrac{1}{\omega^2C^2}\to 0\), so \(I\to\dfrac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R}\). The capacitor barely matters.

    Peak voltages

    \[V_R=RI=\frac{R\,\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\dfrac{1}{\omega^2C^2}}},\qquad V_C=X_C I=\frac{\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\,\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\dfrac{1}{\omega^2C^2}}}\]
    • \(V_R\) follows the current: near \(0\) at low frequency, near \(\mathcal{E}_0\) at high frequency.
    • \(V_C\) does the opposite. At low frequency the denominator is about \(1/(\omega C)\), so \(V_C\approx\mathcal{E}_0\). At high frequency the denominator is about \(R\) while \(1/(\omega C)\to 0\), so \(V_C\approx 0\).

    RC filters and the crossover frequency

    Because \(V_R\) and \(V_C\) respond oppositely to frequency, an RC circuit can sort signals by frequency. Connect a speaker (or any device) across one of the components:

    • High-pass filter: take the output across the resistor. Low-frequency signals give \(V_R\approx 0\) and are blocked; high-frequency signals give \(V_R\approx\mathcal{E}_0\) and go through.
    • Low-pass filter: take the output across the capacitor. Low-frequency signals give \(V_C\approx\mathcal{E}_0\) and go through; high-frequency signals are blocked.

    Crossover frequency

    The crossover is where the two curves meet.

    1. Set the peak voltages equal: \(V_R=V_C\), so \(RI=X_C I\) and \(R=X_C=\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\).
    2. Solve for \(\omega\): \[\omega_c=\frac{1}{RC}=\frac{1}{\tau}\] where \(\tau=RC\) is the time constant you met in DC RC circuits.
    3. In hertz: \(\omega_c=2\pi f_c\), so \(f_c=\dfrac{1}{2\pi RC}\).
    4. At the crossover, \(V_R=V_C=\mathcal{E}_0/\sqrt2\) and \(\varphi=\tan^{-1}(1)=45^\circ\).

    Below \(\omega_c\): \(V_C\approx\mathcal{E}_0\), \(V_R\approx 0\). Above \(\omega_c\): \(V_C\approx 0\), \(V_R\approx\mathcal{E}_0\). The crossover depends only on \(R\) and \(C\), not on the source.

    The series RL circuit

    The analysis follows the same pattern as for the RC circuit.

    1. Sketch the phasor for the current \(I\), at a low angle around \(30^\circ\).
    2. Sketch the resistor voltage phasor \(V_R\), parallel to \(I\): resistor voltage and current are always in step.
    3. Sketch the inductor voltage phasor \(V_L\). The inductor voltage leads the current (ELI), so \(V_L\) is \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise from \(I\).
    4. Add the two voltages with the parallelogram rule, using \(V_R=RI\) and \(V_L=X_L I=\omega L I\).
    5. The length of the sum is the peak source voltage: \(\mathcal{E}_0=\sqrt{V_R^2+V_L^2}=I\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L)^2}\).
    6. The peak current is \[I=\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L)^2}}\]
    7. The phase angle is \(\varphi=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{V_L}{V_R}\right)=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{X_L}{R}\right)\).

    Result. The source phasor is counterclockwise from the current, so the current trails the voltage and we use the \(-\) sign: \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t-\varphi)\), with \(\varphi=\tan^{-1}(X_L/R)\) and impedance \(Z=\sqrt{R^2+X_L^2}\).

    Worked example: an RL circuit

    Question. An AC source of peak voltage \(5.0\ \text{V}\) and frequency \(f=1000\ \text{Hz}\) is connected in series to a resistor \(R=10.0\ \Omega\) and an inductor \(L=4.0\ \text{mH}\). Find the peak current and the phase angle between the current and the source voltage.

    1. Angular frequency: \(\omega=2\pi f=2\pi(1000\ \text{Hz})=6283.2\ \text{rad/s}\).
    2. Calculate \(X_L\) first, for later use: \(X_L=\omega L=(6283.2\ \text{s}^{-1})(4.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{H})=25.13\ \Omega\).
    3. From the phasor triangle, \(\mathcal{E}_0=\sqrt{V_R^2+V_L^2}=\sqrt{(RI)^2+(X_L I)^2}=I\sqrt{R^2+X_L^2}\).
    4. Peak current: \[I=\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+X_L^2}}=\frac{5.0\ \text{V}}{\sqrt{(10.0\ \Omega)^2+(25.13\ \Omega)^2}}=\frac{5.0\ \text{V}}{27.05\ \Omega}=0.18\ \text{A}\]
    5. Phase angle: \(\tan\varphi=\dfrac{V_L}{V_R}=\dfrac{X_L I}{RI}=\dfrac{X_L}{R}=2.513\), so \(\varphi=\tan^{-1}(2.513)=68.3^\circ\) (or \(1.19\ \text{rad}\)).
    6. Going counterclockwise from \(I\) to \(\mathcal{E}_0\), the current trails: \(i(t)=(0.18\ \text{A})\cos(\omega t-68.3^\circ)\).

    Make sure your calculator is in degrees if you want \(\varphi\) in degrees, and in radians if you want it in radians.

    RL phasors in action

    Here the inductor phasor sits \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise from the current, and the source phasor is ahead of the current by \(\varphi\). On the graph, each current peak arrives after the source-voltage peak.

    Try this

    • Raise the frequency. \(X_L=\omega L\) grows, \(\varphi\) approaches \(90^\circ\) and the peak current drops.
    • Find settings where \(X_L=R\) and check that \(\varphi=45^\circ\).
    • Compare with the RC simulation: the triangle is flipped, and the current now trails.

    Checkpoint 6: reactance, current and phase in an RL circuit

    RL filters and the crossover frequency

    Looking at the peak voltages across the resistor and the inductor:

    \[V_R=RI=R\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+X_L^2}}\right)=\frac{R\,\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}}\] \[V_L=X_L I=(\omega L)\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}}\right)=\frac{\omega L\,\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}}\]

    Original Desmos version: RL filter on Desmos (opens in a new tab).

    Filters

    • Low-pass filter: take the output across the resistor. Low-frequency signals give \(V_R\approx\mathcal{E}_0\); high-frequency signals are blocked.
    • High-pass filter: take the output across the inductor. Only high-frequency signals give a large output.

    This is the reverse of the RC circuit, where the resistor gave the high-pass output.

    Crossover frequency

    1. Set the peak voltages equal: \(\dfrac{R\,\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}}=\dfrac{\omega L\,\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}}\).
    2. The denominators are the same, so \(R=\omega L\) and \[\omega_c=\frac{R}{L}\]
    3. An RL circuit has a time constant \(\tau=L/R\) (for RC it was \(\tau=RC\)), which sets how long the current takes to rise in a DC circuit; about \(5\tau\) to reach its maximum, as for RC circuits. Once again, \(\omega_c=1/\tau\).

    The crossover frequency is set by the components alone. It does not depend on the source voltage or frequency.

    Summary

    In both series circuits, the source phasor is the vector sum of a resistor phasor along the current and a reactive phasor at a right angle to it. Every current has the form \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t\pm\varphi)\), using \(+\) when the current is ahead of the voltage and \(-\) when it trails.
