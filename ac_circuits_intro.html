    What is an AC circuit?

    Alternating-current (AC) circuits consist of a time-dependent source connected to components such as resistors, capacitors and inductors. Many time dependences are possible for the source, but here we restrict ourselves to periodic signals with a sinusoidal shape. People call this a “sine wave” even when the function is written as a cosine.

    In a circuit diagram an AC source is drawn as a circle with a small wave inside. Its emf is written \(\mathcal{E}(t)\) to remind us that it changes with time \(t\).

    The central question

    The source voltage swings back and forth between \(+\mathcal{E}_0\) and \(-\mathcal{E}_0\), repeating every period \(T\). Every current and every component voltage in the circuit then oscillates at the same frequency, but not necessarily in step with the source. Analyzing an AC circuit means finding two things for each quantity:

    • its size, the peak value it reaches, and
    • its timing, the phase that says when it reaches that peak compared with the source.

    You will first learn to read a signal on an oscilloscope, then describe it with an equation and with a rotating vector called a phasor, and finally apply these tools to a resistor, a capacitor and an inductor.

    Seeing an AC source on an oscilloscope

    An oscilloscope draws voltage on the vertical axis against time on the horizontal axis. Its screen is a grid of divisions, also called blocks. Two settings turn divisions into physical quantities: seconds per division for the horizontal direction and volts per division for the vertical direction.

    Try this

    • Change the input from a sine wave to a square wave, then to a triangle wave.
    • Vary the frequency and watch how many cycles fit on the screen.
    • Vary the amplitude of the signal with the gain.
    • Adjust the grid used to measure those quantities with the horizontal and vertical offsets.
    • Set the frequency to 500 Hz and the time scale to 1 ms per division. Check that one period covers 2 divisions, so \(T = 2\ \text{ms}\), and that \(f = 1/T = 500\ \text{Hz}\). You could find the frequency from the screen even without knowing it in advance.

    Reading period and peak voltage from the screen

    Every measurement on an oscilloscope is a count of divisions multiplied by a setting.

    \[T = (\text{divisions in one period})\times(\text{seconds per division}),\qquad f=\frac{1}{T}\] \[\mathcal{E}_0 = (\text{divisions from center line to peak})\times(\text{volts per division})\] \[\mathcal{E}_{\text{pp}} = 2\,\mathcal{E}_0 \quad\text{(peak-to-peak voltage)}\]

    Example 1: the period

    Here the signal is set to 500 Hz and the time scale to 1 ms per division. Neighboring peaks are 2 divisions apart, so \(T = (2)(1\ \text{ms}) = 2\ \text{ms}\) and \(f = 1/(0.002\ \text{s}) = 500\ \text{Hz}\).

    Example 2: peak and peak-to-peak voltage

    With 5 V per division, the trace rises 1 division above the center line, so \(\mathcal{E}_0 = (1)(5\ \text{V}) = 5\ \text{V}\). From the top to the bottom of the trace is 2 divisions, so \(\mathcal{E}_{\text{pp}} = 10\ \text{V} = 2\,\mathcal{E}_0\).

    Measuring peak-to-peak is often easier on a real screen: use the vertical offset to put the bottom of the trace on a grid line, count up to the top, then divide by 2 to get the peak voltage.

    Writing the emf as a function of time

    We write the time-dependent emf of an AC source as

    \[\mathcal{E}(t)=\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t)\]

    The constant \(\mathcal{E}_0\) is the maximum value, or peak voltage, and \(\omega\) is the angular frequency. The symbol for the peak voltage varies between authors, but its meaning is always clear from context.

    Why \(\omega = 2\pi/T\)

    1. The cosine repeats whenever its argument grows by \(2\pi\): \(\cos(x+2\pi)=\cos x\).
    2. The emf repeats after one period, so the argument must grow by exactly \(2\pi\) between \(t\) and \(t+T\): \(\omega(t+T)-\omega t = 2\pi\).
    3. That gives \(\omega T = 2\pi\), so \(\omega=\dfrac{2\pi}{T}\). With \(f = \dfrac{1}{T}\), this is also \(\omega = 2\pi f\).

    Root-mean-square value

    The average of \(\mathcal{E}(t)\) over a period is zero, so we need a different measure of a “typical” size. The root-mean-square (rms) value is the square root of the average of \(\mathcal{E}^2\):

    1. \(\mathcal{E}_{\text{rms}}=\sqrt{\langle \mathcal{E}^2\rangle}=\mathcal{E}_0\sqrt{\langle\cos^2(\omega t)\rangle}\)
    2. Using \(\cos^2 x=\tfrac12\left(1+\cos 2x\right)\), the average of \(\cos 2x\) over a period is zero, so \(\langle\cos^2(\omega t)\rangle=\tfrac12\).
    3. Therefore \(\mathcal{E}_{\text{rms}}=\dfrac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt2}\).

    In the United States, outlets provide \(\mathcal{E}_{\text{rms}}=120\ \text{V}\), which corresponds to a peak value \(\mathcal{E}_0=\sqrt2\,(120\ \text{V})\approx 170\ \text{V}\). The peak-to-peak voltage is \(\mathcal{E}_{\text{pp}}=2\,\mathcal{E}_0\).

    Checkpoint 3: matching quantities

    Two pictures of the same signal

    Representation 1: the standard graph

    Plotting \(\mathcal{E}(t)=\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t)\) against time gives a curve whose height above the axis at its top is \(\mathcal{E}_0\) and whose horizontal repeat distance is \(T\).

    Representation 2: the phasor

    A phasor represents a time-dependent quantity as a vector. Picture a vector of length \(\mathcal{E}_0\) that makes an angle \(\theta=\omega t\) with the horizontal axis. As time passes it rotates counterclockwise, completing one turn in each period \(T\). At every instant, the horizontal component of the vector is

    \[\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t)=\mathcal{E}(t)\]

    so the instantaneous emf is the projection of the phasor onto the horizontal axis. Technically the phasor keeps rotating, but for convenience diagrams are often drawn with it in the first quadrant.

    Try this

    • Move the time slider to \(t=T/4\). The phasor points straight up, its horizontal projection is zero, and the graph crosses zero at the same moment.
    • Move to \(t=T/2\). The phasor points left and the emf equals \(-\mathcal{E}_0\).
    • Change the period and notice that one full turn of the phasor always matches one full cycle on the graph.

    A resistor connected to an AC source

    Consider a circuit made of one AC source connected to one resistor.

    The task is to find the current at every instant. For reasons that become clear with the capacitor, we write the general current as

    \[i(t)=I\cos(\omega t\pm\varphi)\]

    where \(I\) is the peak current and \(\varphi\ge 0\) is the phase angle, which measures the time shift between the moment the current peaks and the moment the voltage peaks.

    Sign convention. We use \(+\varphi\) when the current is ahead of (leads) the voltage, and \(-\varphi\) when the current trails (lags) the voltage.

    Derivation

    1. Ohm’s law at every instant: \(v_R(t)=R\,i(t)=V_R\cos(\omega t\pm\varphi)\), with peak voltage \(V_R=RI\).
    2. The resistor is connected directly across the source, so the loop rule gives \(\mathcal{E}(t)=v_R(t)\) at every instant.
    3. So \(\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t)=V_R\cos(\omega t\pm\varphi)\) for all \(t\). Two cosines can agree at all times only if their amplitudes and phases match: \(V_R=\mathcal{E}_0\) and \(\varphi=0\). With no shift, the current neither leads nor trails, and the sign does not matter.
    4. The current is therefore \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t)=\dfrac{V_R}{R}\cos(\omega t)\).

    Result. For a resistor, current and voltage are in phase: they peak at the same instant, and their phasors point in the same direction.

    Checkpoint 4: match voltages to times

    A capacitor connected to an AC source

    A capacitor connected to an AC source charges, discharges and charges again in the opposite direction, over and over. The current in the wires is the rate at which charge arrives on the plates.

    Derivation

    1. Loop rule: the capacitor is directly across the source, so \(v_C(t)=\mathcal{E}(t)=\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t)\), and the peak capacitor voltage is \(V_C=\mathcal{E}_0\).
    2. Charge on the plates: \(q(t)=C\,v_C(t)=C\,\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t)\).
    3. Current is the rate of change of charge: \(i(t)=\dfrac{dq}{dt}=-\omega C\,\mathcal{E}_0\sin(\omega t)\).
    4. Use the identity \(-\sin x=\cos\!\left(x+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\): \(i(t)=\omega C\,\mathcal{E}_0\cos\!\left(\omega t+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\).
    5. Define the capacitive reactance \(X_C=\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\). The peak current is then \(I=\omega C\,V_C=\dfrac{V_C}{X_C}\).

    Result. \(i(t)=I\cos\!\left(\omega t+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\) with \(I=V_C/X_C\). Compared with \(i=I\cos(\omega t\pm\varphi)\), the \(+\) sign applies with \(\varphi=\pi/2\): the current leads the voltage by a quarter period, \(T/4\). In the phasor picture, the current phasor is \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise ahead of the voltage phasor.

    Making sense of \(X_C\)

    • Units: \(\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\) has units \(\dfrac{1}{(\text{s}^{-1})(\text{C/V})}=\dfrac{\text{V}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{C}}=\dfrac{\text{V}}{\text{A}}=\Omega\), the same as resistance.
    • Low frequency, \(\omega\to 0\): \(X_C\to\infty\). A capacitor blocks steady (DC) current.
    • High frequency, \(\omega\to\infty\): \(X_C\to 0\). The plates barely charge before the current reverses, so the capacitor behaves almost like a wire.

    Checkpoint 6: current at a given instant

    What is an inductor?

    Inductors are devices that limit changes in the current in a circuit. They look like small coils of wire. A current in a wire produces a magnetic field, and that field stores energy as magnetic energy, much as a capacitor stores electric potential energy in the electric field between its plates. Magnetic fields come later in the course; for now, the essential point is that when an inductor is present, a voltage appears across its ends:

    \[v_L = L\,\frac{di}{dt}\]

    Here \(L\) is the inductance, a property of the inductor in the same way capacitance is a property of a capacitor and resistance is a property of a resistor. Inductance is measured in henries (H). Its value depends on the geometry of the coil and the material it is made of, just as capacitance and resistance depend on geometry and material. You do not need those details here.

    What the equation tells you

    • If the current is constant, \(\dfrac{di}{dt}=0\) and the voltage across the inductor is zero. In steady DC circuits an inductor acts like a plain wire.
    • In DC circuits, inductors matter only when a switch opens or closes. Closing a switch makes the current rise briefly from zero to its final value; opening it makes the current drop to zero very quickly, and the large \(di/dt\) creates a voltage that can cause a small spark (arcing) at the switch.
    • In an AC circuit the current changes all the time, so an inductor always has a voltage across it. That is the case studied next.

    For more background, see the Wikipedia article on inductors (opens in a new tab).

    An inductor connected to an AC source

    At every instant the voltage across the inductor equals the voltage of the source, so \(v_L(t)=\mathcal{E}(t)=\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t)\). Calling the peak voltage \(V_L=\mathcal{E}_0\), we write \(v_L(t)=V_L\cos(\omega t)\). We look for a current of the form \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t\pm\varphi)\), with \(+\) if the current leads the voltage and \(-\) if it trails, and need to find \(I\), \(\varphi\) and the sign.

    Derivation

    1. Combine the inductor equation with the source voltage: \(L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}=V_L\cos(\omega t)\), so \(\dfrac{di}{dt}=\left(\dfrac{V_L}{L}\right)\cos(\omega t)\).
    2. Integrate with respect to time (an AC current has no constant offset, so the integration constant is zero): \(i(t)=\left(\dfrac{V_L}{\omega L}\right)\sin(\omega t)\).
    3. Use the identity \(\sin x=\cos\!\left(x-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\) to compare with the source: \(i(t)=\left(\dfrac{V_L}{\omega L}\right)\cos\!\left(\omega t-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\).
    4. Reading off the amplitude and phase: \(I=\dfrac{V_L}{\omega L}\) and \(\varphi=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\), with the \(-\) sign because the current trails the voltage.
    5. Following the same pattern as for the capacitor, define the inductive reactance \(X_L=\omega L\). Then \(I=\dfrac{V_L}{X_L}\), or \(V_L=I X_L\), which looks like Ohm’s law.

    Result. \(i(t)=I\cos\!\left(\omega t-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\) with \(I=V_L/X_L\) and \(X_L=\omega L\). The current lags the voltage by \(\pi/2\), a quarter period: it peaks \(T/4\) after the voltage does. This is the opposite of the capacitor, where the current leads.

    Making sense of \(X_L\)

    • \(X_L=\omega L\) grows linearly with the angular frequency and is measured in ohms, like a resistance or a capacitive reactance.
    • Low frequency, \(\omega\to 0\): \(X_L\to 0\). With slowly changing current, the inductor behaves like a wire.
    • High frequency, \(\omega\to\infty\): \(X_L\to\infty\). The inductor strongly opposes rapid changes in current.

    Checkpoint 7: inductive reactance

    Phasor representation for the inductor

    When an inductor is connected alone to an AC source, the equations for the circuit are:

    • Instantaneous voltage of the source: \(\mathcal{E}(t)=\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t)\)
    • Instantaneous voltage across the inductor: \(v_L(t)=V_L\cos(\omega t)\), with peak value \(V_L=\mathcal{E}_0\)
    • Instantaneous current in the circuit: \(i(t)=I\cos\!\left(\omega t-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\)
    • Peak current: \(I=\dfrac{V_L}{X_L}\), where \(X_L=\omega L\) is the inductive reactance

    The voltage phasor makes angle \(\omega t\) with the horizontal axis. The current phasor makes angle \(\omega t-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), so it trails the voltage phasor by a right angle as both rotate counterclockwise.

    A way to remember the two reactive components: in a capacitor the current is \(90^\circ\) ahead of the voltage; in an inductor it is \(90^\circ\) behind.

    Summary

    Each component connected alone across a source \(\mathcal{E}(t)=\mathcal{E}_0\cos(\omega t)\) has the full source voltage across it. What changes is the size of the current and its timing. Every current has the form \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t\pm\varphi)\), using \(+\) when the current is ahead of the voltage and \(-\) when it trails the voltage.

    Remembering who leads: “ELI the ICE man”

    The phrase gives the order of voltage and current for the two reactive components. Read each word from left to right; the first letter of the pair leads the second.

    • ELI: in an inductor (L), the voltage (E) comes before the current (I). The voltage leads the current, so the current trails it by \(90^\circ\): \(i(t)=I\cos\!\left(\omega t-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\).
    • ICE: in a capacitor (C), the current (I) comes before the voltage (E). The voltage trails the current, so the current leads by \(90^\circ\): \(i(t)=I\cos\!\left(\omega t+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\).

    A resistor has no shift at all: voltage and current peak together.
