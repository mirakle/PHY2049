    The series RLC circuit

    You have met each component alone, then in pairs. Now all three share one loop: a resistor, an inductor and a capacitor in series with an AC source. This is the circuit behind radio tuners, wireless chargers and the capacitor banks that power companies bolt onto factories.

    2. Define the impedance and work through a full numerical example, then solve one yourself.
    3. Discover resonance: why the current suddenly peaks at one special frequency.
    4. Follow where the energy goes: power in each component, the power factor, and how engineers correct it.

    One loop, three components

    Two facts organize everything that follows:

    • One current. The components are in series, so the same current \(i(t)\) flows through all three at every instant.
    • Voltages add. The loop rule gives \(v_R(t)+v_L(t)+v_C(t)=\mathcal{E}(t)\) at every instant.

    Each component treats that common current its own way, exactly as when it was alone:

    Each component in the loop
    ComponentPeak voltagePhasor relative to the current
    Resistor\(V_R=RI\)Parallel to \(I\): in phase
    Inductor\(V_L=X_L I\), \(X_L=\omega L\)\(90^\circ\) counterclockwise from \(I\): voltage leads (ELI)
    Capacitor\(V_C=X_C I\), \(X_C=\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\)\(90^\circ\) clockwise from \(I\): voltage trails (ICE)

    Notice that \(V_L\) and \(V_C\) point in opposite directions. That single fact is the source of everything interesting about this circuit.

    Building the phasor diagram

    Because the current is common to all three components, it is the natural reference. The recipe:

    1. Current first. Draw the current phasor \(I\) at a convenient low angle, about \(30^\circ\). Its actual angle does not matter; only relative angles do.
    2. Resistor. Draw \(V_R=RI\) along \(I\).
    3. Inductor. Draw \(V_L=X_L I\) at \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise from \(I\).
    4. Capacitor. Draw \(V_C=X_C I\) at \(90^\circ\) clockwise from \(I\).
    5. Combine the reactive voltages. \(V_L\) and \(V_C\) lie on the same line in opposite directions, so they reduce to a single phasor of length \(|V_L-V_C|\), pointing toward the larger one.
    6. Add. \(V_R\) and \(V_L-V_C\) are perpendicular. Their vector sum is the source phasor, of length \(\mathcal{E}_0\). The angle from \(I\) to \(\mathcal{E}_0\) is the phase angle \(\varphi\).

    How the current relates to the source voltage

    The finished diagram is a right triangle: one leg \(V_R\), the other leg \(V_L-V_C\), hypotenuse \(\mathcal{E}_0\).

    Magnitude

    1. Pythagoras: \(\mathcal{E}_0^2=V_R^2+(V_L-V_C)^2\).
    2. Substitute \(V_R=RI\), \(V_L=X_LI\), \(V_C=X_CI\): \(\mathcal{E}_0^2=I^2\left[R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2\right]\).
    3. Take the square root: \[I=\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2}}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\sqrt{R^2+\left(\omega L-\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}}\]

    Phase

    1. In the triangle, \(\tan\varphi=\dfrac{|V_L-V_C|}{V_R}=\dfrac{|X_L-X_C|}{R}\), so \(\varphi=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{|X_L-X_C|}{R}\right)\), with \(0\le\varphi<90^\circ\).
    2. Which way? Look at where \(\mathcal{E}_0\) sits relative to \(I\):
      • If \(X_L>X_C\), the reactive leg points up (counterclockwise from \(I\)). The source voltage is ahead, so the current trails: \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t-\varphi)\). The circuit behaves inductively.
      • If \(X_LThe current leads: \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t+\varphi)\). The circuit behaves capacitively.
      • If \(X_L=X_C\), \(\varphi=0\): current and voltage are in phase. Keep this case in mind; it gets its own section.

    Same convention as before: \(\varphi\) is a positive angle, and the sign in \(i(t)=I\cos(\omega t\pm\varphi)\) is \(+\) when the current is ahead of the source voltage and \(-\) when it trails. Some books instead write \(\varphi=\tan^{-1}\!\big((X_L-X_C)/R\big)\) with a sign and always use \(\cos(\omega t-\varphi)\); both describe the same current.

    Impedance: the circuit’s total opposition

    The peak current has the form of Ohm’s law, \(I=\mathcal{E}_0/Z\), with

    \[Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2}=\sqrt{R^2+\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}\]

    \(Z\) is the impedance, measured in ohms. Divide every side of the voltage triangle by \(I\) and you get the impedance triangle: legs \(R\) and \(X_L-X_C\), hypotenuse \(Z\), and the same angle \(\varphi\). So also

    \[\cos\varphi=\frac{R}{Z},\qquad \sin\varphi=\frac{|X_L-X_C|}{Z}\]

    Things to notice

    • Resistance and reactance do not simply add; they combine at right angles.
    • The two reactances partly cancel each other because they enter as a difference. Adding a capacitor to an inductive circuit can lower the impedance.
    • \(Z\ge R\) always, with equality only when \(X_L=X_C\).
    • Special cases recover earlier results. With no capacitor effect (\(X_C=0\)), \(Z=\sqrt{R^2+X_L^2}\): the RL circuit. With no inductor (\(X_L=0\)), \(Z=\sqrt{R^2+X_C^2}\): the RC circuit.

    Worked example: a complete RLC calculation

    The circuit. Frequency \(f=500\ \text{Hz}\), peak source voltage \(\mathcal{E}_0=10\ \text{V}\), \(R=8\ \Omega\), \(L=5\ \text{mH}\), \(C=30\ \mu\text{F}\). Find the angular frequency, both reactances, the impedance, the peak current and the phase.

    1. Angular frequency. \(\omega=2\pi f=2\pi(500\ \text{Hz})=3141.59\ \text{rad/s}\).
    2. Inductive reactance. \(X_L=\omega L=(3141.59\ \text{s}^{-1})(5\times10^{-3}\ \text{H})=15.71\ \Omega\).
    3. Capacitive reactance. \(X_C=\dfrac{1}{\omega C}=\dfrac{1}{(3141.59\ \text{s}^{-1})(30\times10^{-6}\ \text{F})}=10.61\ \Omega\).
    4. Impedance. \(X_L-X_C=5.10\ \Omega\), so \(Z=\sqrt{8^2+5.10^2}\ \Omega=\sqrt{90.0}\ \Omega=9.49\ \Omega\).
    5. Peak current. \(I=\dfrac{\mathcal{E}_0}{Z}=\dfrac{10\ \text{V}}{9.49\ \Omega}=1.05\ \text{A}\).
    6. Phase. \(\varphi=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{5.10}{8}\right)=0.57\ \text{rad}=32.5^\circ\). Since \(X_L>X_C\), the current trails: \(i(t)=(1.05\ \text{A})\cos(\omega t-0.57)\).

    Check with the voltages. \(V_R=8.43\ \text{V}\), \(V_L=16.56\ \text{V}\), \(V_C=11.19\ \text{V}\). The inductor alone carries more than the source’s 10 V peak, yet \(\sqrt{8.43^2+(16.56-11.19)^2}=10.0\ \text{V}\). Peak voltages never add like ordinary numbers, because they peak at different times.

    Your turn: an RLC circuit

    Resonance: why it happens

    The reactances pull in opposite directions as the frequency changes:

    • \(X_L=\omega L\) grows with \(\omega\): an inductor fights fast changes of current.
    • \(X_C=\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\) shrinks with \(\omega\): a capacitor barely charges before the current reverses.

    One rising curve and one falling curve must cross. At the crossing, \(X_L=X_C\), the reactive term in the impedance vanishes, and \(Z\) drops to its smallest possible value, \(R\).

    Finding the resonance frequency

    1. Set the reactances equal: \(\omega_0 L=\dfrac{1}{\omega_0 C}\).
    2. Solve: \(\omega_0^2=\dfrac{1}{LC}\), so \[\omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad f_0=\frac{\omega_0}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\]
    3. Calculus check: \(Z(\omega)^2=R^2+\left(\omega L-\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2\). The first term is fixed and the second is a square, never negative, so \(Z\) is smallest exactly when \(\omega L-1/(\omega C)=0\). Equivalently, \(\dfrac{d}{d\omega}Z^2=2\left(\omega L-\dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)\left(L+\dfrac{1}{\omega^2C}\right)=0\) only at \(\omega_0\), since the second factor is always positive.

    What happens at \(\omega_0\)

    • \(Z=R\), so the peak current reaches its maximum, \(I_{\max}=\mathcal{E}_0/R\).
    • \(\varphi=0\): current and source voltage are in phase, and \(\mathcal{E}_0\) lies along \(I\).
    • \(V_L\) and \(V_C\) are equal and opposite, so they cancel in the sum. Individually, though, each can be much larger than \(\mathcal{E}_0\).
    • \(\omega_0\) depends only on \(L\) and \(C\). The resistance sets how tall and how sharp the current peak is, not where it is.

    The deeper reason: a natural oscillation

    Remove the source and the resistor and you have an LC circuit: charge sloshes back and forth, energy trading between the capacitor’s electric field and the inductor’s magnetic field, at exactly \(\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}\). (This is the electrical twin of a mass on a spring, \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\).) Driving an RLC circuit at \(\omega_0\) is like pushing a child on a swing at the swing’s own rhythm: each push adds energy in step with the motion, and the response is largest. The resistor plays the role of friction.

    Try this

    • Change \(R\) only. The peak stays at the same \(\omega_0\), but a small \(R\) makes it higher (\(I_{\max}=\mathcal{E}_0/R\); watch the axis labels, which rescale so the peak always fits) and narrower, while a large \(R\) flattens it.
    • Change \(L\) or \(C\). The peak slides left when either grows, as \(\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}\) predicts.
    • Move the probe away from \(\omega_0\) on either side and watch the phase: current leads below resonance, trails above.

    Sharpness. The narrowness of the peak is measured by the quality factor \(Q=\dfrac{\omega_0 L}{R}\). A radio needs a high \(Q\) so that it responds to one station and ignores its neighbors on the dial.

    Worked example: the same circuit at resonance

    Take the earlier circuit (\(\mathcal{E}_0=10\ \text{V}\), \(R=8\ \Omega\), \(L=5\ \text{mH}\), \(C=30\ \mu\text{F}\)) and tune the source to its resonance frequency.

    1. Resonance angular frequency. \(\omega_0=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(5\times10^{-3})(30\times10^{-6})}}=2582\ \text{rad/s}\), so \(f_0=\dfrac{\omega_0}{2\pi}=410.9\ \text{Hz}\).
    2. Reactances. \(X_L=\omega_0 L=12.91\ \Omega\) and \(X_C=\dfrac{1}{\omega_0 C}=12.91\ \Omega\). Equal, as they must be.
    3. Impedance. \(Z=\sqrt{8^2+0^2}=8.00\ \Omega=R\).
    4. Peak current. \(I=\dfrac{10\ \text{V}}{8\ \Omega}=1.25\ \text{A}\), up from 1.05 A at 500 Hz.
    5. Phase. \(\varphi=\tan^{-1}(0/8)=0\): current in phase with the source.
    6. The surprise. \(V_L=V_C=(1.25\ \text{A})(12.91\ \Omega)=16.1\ \text{V}\), more than one and a half times the source’s 10 V peak. In high-\(Q\) circuits this voltage magnification can reach hundreds of times the source voltage, which is why components in resonant circuits need high voltage ratings.

    Your turn: phasors at and near resonance

    Where does the energy go?

    The instantaneous power delivered to a component is \(p(t)=v(t)\,i(t)\). What matters for heating, bills and batteries is the average over one period, \(\langle p\rangle=\dfrac1T\displaystyle\int_0^T p(t)\,dt\). Write the current as \(i=I\cos\theta\) with \(\theta=\omega t\mp\varphi\).

    Resistor

    \(v_R=RI\cos\theta\), so \(p_R=I^2R\cos^2\theta\). This is never negative: a resistor only takes energy. Using \(\cos^2\theta=\tfrac12(1+\cos2\theta)\) and the fact that \(\cos2\theta\) averages to zero, \[\langle p_R\rangle=\tfrac12 I^2R=I_{\text{rms}}^2R\]

    Inductor and capacitor

    \(v_L=X_LI\cos(\theta+\tfrac{\pi}{2})=-X_LI\sin\theta\), so \(p_L=-I^2X_L\sin\theta\cos\theta=-\tfrac12I^2X_L\sin2\theta\). Similarly \(p_C=+\tfrac12I^2X_C\sin2\theta\). A full period of \(\sin2\theta\) averages to zero: \[\langle p_L\rangle=\langle p_C\rangle=0\]

    Inductors and capacitors store energy for a quarter cycle (in the magnetic field \(\tfrac12Li^2\), in the electric field \(\tfrac12Cv^2\)) and hand it back the next quarter cycle. They borrow; they never consume.

    The source

    Energy is conserved, so the source’s average output equals what the resistor dissipates: \(\langle p\rangle=\tfrac12I^2R\). Using \(I=\mathcal{E}_0/Z\) and \(R/Z=\cos\varphi\): \[\langle p\rangle=\tfrac12\,\mathcal{E}_0\,I\cos\varphi=\mathcal{E}_{\text{rms}}\,I_{\text{rms}}\cos\varphi\]

    The power factor

    The product \(\mathcal{E}_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\) is called the apparent power. Only the fraction \(\cos\varphi\) of it is actually delivered on average. That fraction is the power factor:

    \[\text{power factor}=\cos\varphi=\frac{R}{Z},\qquad 0<\cos\varphi\le1\]
    • \(\cos\varphi=1\) (purely resistive, or at resonance): every bit of current does useful work.
    • \(\cos\varphi\) small (strongly reactive): a large current flows back and forth, mostly ferrying energy into and out of fields, while little is delivered.

    Why anyone cares

    The power company must deliver a given power \(P\) at a fixed voltage. The current it must push is \(I_{\text{rms}}=\dfrac{P}{\mathcal{E}_{\text{rms}}\cos\varphi}\). A power factor of 0.7 means about 43% more current than necessary. The transmission lines have resistance, and their losses go as \(I^2\), so that extra current roughly doubles the line losses, and every wire and transformer must be oversized to carry it.

    In the real world

    • Factories. Electric motors, compressors and welders are coils: highly inductive loads with lagging power factors, often 0.7 to 0.85. Utilities charge industrial customers penalty fees when the power factor drops below a threshold, commonly around 0.9 to 0.95.
    • Your laptop charger. Many countries require power-factor-correction circuits in power supplies above roughly 75 W, so millions of chargers do not each draw wasteful current.
    • Substations. Utilities install large capacitor banks to cancel the inductive load of entire neighborhoods.

    Correcting the power factor with a capacitor

    Most real loads are inductive (\(X_L>X_C\)). Since capacitive and inductive effects cancel, the fix is to add capacitance. There are two ways to connect it.

    Option 1: a capacitor in series

    1. Goal: \(\cos\varphi=1\), which needs \(X_L=X_{C,\text{total}}\), i.e. resonance at the operating frequency.
    2. Required total capacitance: \(\dfrac{1}{\omega C_{\text{tot}}}=X_L\Rightarrow C_{\text{tot}}=\dfrac{1}{\omega X_L}=\dfrac{1}{\omega^2L}\).
    3. Capacitors in series combine as \(\dfrac{1}{C_{\text{tot}}}=\dfrac{1}{C}+\dfrac{1}{C'}\), so the added capacitor is \[C'=\left(\frac{1}{C_{\text{tot}}}-\frac{1}{C}\right)^{-1}\]
    4. This only works when \(X_L>X_C\): a series capacitor lowers the total capacitance and raises \(X_C\). If the circuit is already capacitive, you would need more inductance instead.

    Series correction changes the current through the load, so it suits small circuits and tuned systems. It is rarely used on power grids.

    Option 2: a capacitor in parallel (what industry does)

    1. A capacitor \(C'\) placed directly across the source sees the full source voltage, and the RLC branch keeps exactly the same voltage, current and power as before. Nothing about the load changes.
    2. Load branch: peak current \(I=\mathcal{E}_0/Z\), trailing the voltage by \(\varphi\). Split its phasor into a part along \(\mathcal{E}_0\), \(I\cos\varphi\), and a part \(90^\circ\) behind it, \(I\sin\varphi\).
    3. Capacitor branch: peak current \(I'=\omega C'\mathcal{E}_0\), \(90^\circ\) ahead of the voltage (ICE).
    4. The source supplies the sum. Choose \(C'\) so the capacitor cancels the out-of-phase part: \(\omega C'\mathcal{E}_0=I\sin\varphi\).
    5. With \(I=\mathcal{E}_0/Z\) and \(\sin\varphi=(X_L-X_C)/Z\): \[C'=\frac{X_L-X_C}{\omega Z^2}\]
    6. The source current drops to \(I\cos\varphi\), now in phase with the voltage. Same power delivered, less current in the supply lines.

    Analogy. Think of the reactive current as a delivery truck driving loaded boxes out and empty boxes back all day. The parallel capacitor is a local warehouse next to the load: the boxes now shuttle between warehouse and load, and the long road from the power plant only carries what is actually used.

    Worked example: power and its correction

    Back to the circuit at \(f=500\ \text{Hz}\): \(\mathcal{E}_0=10\ \text{V}\), \(R=8\ \Omega\), \(L=5\ \text{mH}\), \(C=30\ \mu\text{F}\). From before: \(\omega=3141.59\ \text{rad/s}\), \(X_L=15.71\ \Omega\), \(X_C=10.61\ \Omega\), \(Z=9.49\ \Omega\), \(I=1.054\ \text{A}\).

    1. Power factor. \(\cos\varphi=\dfrac{R}{Z}=\dfrac{8}{9.49}=0.843\).
    2. Average power from the source. \(P=\tfrac12\mathcal{E}_0I\cos\varphi=\tfrac12(10)(1.054)(0.843)=4.44\ \text{W}\).
    3. Check in the resistor. \(\tfrac12I^2R=\tfrac12(1.054)^2(8)=4.44\ \text{W}\). The inductor and capacitor average 0 W.
    4. Series correction. \(C_{\text{tot}}=\dfrac{1}{\omega X_L}=\dfrac{1}{(3141.59)(15.71)}=20.26\ \mu\text{F}\), then \(C'=\left(\dfrac{1}{20.26}-\dfrac{1}{30}\right)^{-1}\mu\text{F}=62.4\ \mu\text{F}\).
    5. Parallel correction. \(C'=\dfrac{X_L-X_C}{\omega Z^2}=\dfrac{5.10}{(3141.59)(90.0)}=18.0\ \mu\text{F}\). The source current drops from 1.054 A to \(I\cos\varphi=0.889\ \text{A}\) while the power stays 4.44 W.
